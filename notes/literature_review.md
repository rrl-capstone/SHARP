# Literature Review: Multimorbidity Care Quality in People With and Without HIV

## 1. Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV infection from a terminal illness into a chronic, manageable condition, and people with HIV (PWH) in high-income settings now approach near-normal life expectancy. This success has produced a demographic shift: a growing population of PWH surviving into middle and older age. Alongside this shift, an independent and now well-replicated body of evidence has emerged showing that PWH develop chronic, age-associated comorbidities — cardiovascular disease, diabetes, renal disease, bone disease, and others — earlier in life than the general population, and increasingly carry multiple such conditions simultaneously (multimorbidity) alongside the polypharmacy that managing them requires.

Having a diagnosed condition and receiving appropriate ongoing care for it are distinct questions, however. Whether PWH who develop these age-related comorbidities earlier are also being monitored and treated to the same standard as demographically similar HIV-negative peers is comparatively understudied. This review synthesizes three streams of literature relevant to that question: (1) epidemiological evidence establishing premature multimorbidity in HIV, (2) methodologies for quantifying overall comorbidity and frailty burden from administrative/claims data, and (3) methodologies for measuring condition-specific quality of care via process-of-care indicators. Together, these motivate and inform a proposed matched-cohort study comparing multimorbidity patterns and care-quality trajectories between people with and without HIV.

## 2. Background and Foundations

A few concepts recur throughout this literature. **Multimorbidity** refers to the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions in one person. A **comorbidity index** condenses a patient's overall disease burden into a single weighted score, typically for risk adjustment or mortality prediction. **Frailty** describes a state of increased vulnerability to adverse outcomes that correlates with, but is conceptually distinct from, multimorbidity. A **process-of-care quality indicator** is an explicit, evidence-based rule specifying what care *should* happen for a given condition (e.g., "patients with diabetes should have HbA1c checked annually"), used to audit whether it actually did.

Three works anchor the literature reviewed here. Charlson et al. (1987) introduced the first widely adopted comorbidity index, deriving 19 weighted conditions from chart review to predict one-year mortality; it remains the default comorbidity-adjustment tool across health services research four decades later. The Assessing Care of Vulnerable Elders (ACOVE) project (Wenger & Shekelle, 2001) established the dominant framework for process-of-care quality measurement in complex, multimorbid, older populations, using structured expert panels to translate clinical guidelines into explicit, auditable indicators. In HIV research specifically, Guaraldi et al. (2011) is widely credited as the first study to empirically demonstrate, via a direct HIV+/HIV− comparison, that comorbidities traditionally associated with old age appear substantially earlier in PWH — a finding that has since anchored an entire subfield.

## 3. Main Body

### 3.1 Epidemiological Evidence for Premature Multimorbidity in HIV

The core empirical claim motivating this project — that HIV infection is associated with earlier onset of age-related disease — rests on convergent evidence from independent cohorts rather than a single study. Guaraldi et al. (2011), working from a single Italian clinic, found that HIV+ patients aged 41–50 carried the same rate of concurrent conditions as HIV− controls a full decade older. Hasse et al. (2011), using the Swiss HIV Cohort Study, confirmed rising non-AIDS morbidity with age within a PWH-only sample. Schouten et al. (2014), using the Dutch AGEhIV cohort with closely matched controls, found HIV independently associated with comorbidity risk after adjusting for standard risk factors. That three cohorts drawn from three different countries, using three different designs, converge on the same pattern is the strongest form of evidence this literature offers. More recently, Marcus et al. (2020), using 16 years of data from a large U.S. insured population, showed the pattern has persisted despite major ART advances: while the overall life-expectancy gap between PWH and HIV− peers has narrowed, the *comorbidity-free* life-expectancy gap has not — PWH still reach a given disease burden roughly 16 years earlier (9.5 years with early ART initiation).

This literature is not without an open methodological tension worth naming rather than glossing over. Cross-sectional comparisons of this kind cannot fully separate a true biological effect of HIV (accelerated aging, chronic inflammation) from a detection-bias effect: PWH in regular care interact with the health system far more frequently than matched general-population controls, creating more opportunities for a condition to be caught and diagnosed. The literature reviewed here does not resolve this tension, and any study design building on it should treat the magnitude of "premature aging" as partly, not wholly, attributable to HIV pathophysiology.

Part of the effect is plausibly mediated by a specific, modifiable behavior rather than HIV biology directly: Mdodo et al. (2015) found cigarette smoking roughly twice as prevalent among PWH as the general population, with correspondingly lower quit rates — a pattern directly relevant given smoking's established role in several of the conditions driving the broader multimorbidity signal (cardiovascular disease, COPD). Multimorbidity in this population also does not occur in isolation: Guaraldi et al. (2018), using the Italian GEPPO cohort, tied rising multimorbidity directly to rising polypharmacy, while Greene et al. (2015) extended the picture further to geriatric syndromes and frailty, finding the majority of older PWH in their cohort already prefrail. Taken together, this sub-literature suggests that comorbidity count, medication burden, and frailty are three facets of one underlying phenomenon in this population, not independent concerns — any study examining one without the others risks an incomplete picture. High et al. (2012), a consensus report commissioned by the NIH Office of AIDS Research, formally identified this gap over a decade ago, naming the intersection of aging, multimorbidity, and HIV as a critical research priority — a call this literature has substantially answered on the epidemiological side, but, as Section 3.3 argues, not yet on the care-quality side.

### 3.2 Measuring Comorbidity Burden from Administrative Data

Quantifying "how much" comorbidity burden a patient carries requires a validated, computable index. Charlson et al. (1987) remains foundational, but its 19 conditions were originally defined via chart review, not billing codes, and a clinically sound index is only as useful as its administrative implementation. Quan et al. (2005) closed that gap, developing and validating ICD-9-CM and ICD-10 coding algorithms that operationalize Charlson (and the related Elixhauser) comorbidities directly from claims data, with performance matching or exceeding earlier coding algorithms.

A comparative point worth surfacing explicitly, since it is rarely addressed directly in the broader literature: AIDS is itself one of Charlson's 19 scored conditions, and carries the single highest weight in the index (six points, tied only with metastatic solid tumor). Any study comparing Charlson scores between an HIV-positive and an HIV-negative group is therefore comparing a score that is partly defined by the very exposure under study — a structural confound rather than a measurement error, and one this review did not find an established consensus solution for in the reviewed literature. Studies making HIV-status comparisons using Charlson should address this directly (for example, via a modified score excluding the AIDS component) rather than report the standard score unmodified.

A related but distinct construct — medication regimen burden — has its own measurement literature. Hirsch et al. (2014) validated the Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI), a more granular alternative to simple drug-class counts, using claims data across several chronic-disease cohorts including HIV. This offers a possible refinement path for future work, though it is not the approach adopted in the current protocol.

### 3.3 Measuring Quality of Care via Process Indicators

The ACOVE project (Wenger & Shekelle, 2001) supplies the methodological lineage for measuring whether comorbidities are being well-managed, not merely whether they are present. Vulnerable patients were operationally defined using the VES-13 screening survey; a national expert panel identified conditions of concern; and, for each, structured literature review combined with the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method (two rounds of anonymous expert rating with discussion between rounds) produced explicit, auditable process-of-care rules spanning four domains: screening/prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. The project's central empirical finding is itself a significant result: applied to real patients, only about 55% of recommended care processes were actually delivered, ranging from as low as 9% (end-of-life care) to as high as 82% (stroke care) — demonstrating that quality gaps are the norm, not the exception, and vary enormously by condition. The 2007 ACOVE-3 revision (Wenger, Roth & Shekelle, 2007) expanded this to 26 conditions and 392 indicators, and a subsequent systematic review (Askari et al., 2011) confirmed the framework has been successfully adapted across at least 17 independent studies and multiple countries since, establishing it as a durable methodology rather than a one-time project.

This is also where the clearest gap in the literature sits. Despite ACOVE's broad adaptation record, this review found no evidence of ACOVE-style, condition-specific quality indicators having been applied to compare people with and without HIV specifically. Compounding this, ACOVE's validated population (VES-13-defined "vulnerable elders," generally 65 and older) does not fully overlap with the population the epidemiological literature in Section 3.1 identifies as most relevant — PWH reaching comparable disease burden in their late 40s and 50s. Whether a quality-indicator methodology validated in a 65+ general population transfers cleanly to a younger-onset, HIV-specific vulnerable population is an open question this literature has not yet addressed.

## 4. Discussion

The three streams reviewed here are each independently mature but have not previously been combined. The epidemiological literature (3.1) robustly establishes *why* HIV status should predict earlier and heavier multimorbidity. The comorbidity-measurement literature (3.2) provides validated tools to quantify *how much* burden exists, carrying an HIV-specific confound that existing work has not resolved. The quality-indicator literature (3.3) provides a validated methodology for asking *whether* that burden is being adequately managed — but this methodology has not yet been pointed at an HIV-positive population.

Four specific gaps follow directly from this synthesis: (1) the detection-bias-versus-true-acceleration question in the premature-aging literature remains empirically unresolved; (2) no consensus approach exists for handling Charlson's built-in AIDS weighting in HIV-comparative research; (3) ACOVE-style quality indicators have not been validated in a PWH-specific population, nor in a primarily sub-65 vulnerable population more broadly; and (4) HIV case-ascertainment methodology in administrative data varies in whether pharmacy/ART evidence is incorporated alongside diagnosis codes, a choice with direct, documented sensitivity/precision tradeoffs.

These gaps point toward a clear direction for future research: a matched-cohort design directly comparing condition-specific, process-of-care quality measures between PWH and demographically similar HIV-negative controls, using a comorbidity index adapted to avoid the HIV-specific double-counting problem identified above, and an HIV case-finding approach whose evidentiary basis (diagnosis-only versus diagnosis-plus-pharmacy) is stated and justified explicitly rather than assumed.

## 5. Conclusion

Premature multimorbidity in people with HIV is a well-documented, cross-nationally replicated phenomenon, robust across cohort designs and still persistent in the most recent large-scale data available. The tools to measure both overall comorbidity burden and condition-specific care quality are themselves mature and well-validated in their own right, but each carries a limitation specific to this population: Charlson's AIDS weighting structurally confounds HIV-status comparisons, and ACOVE's quality-indicator framework has never been applied to, or validated for, people with HIV. No study identified in this review has combined these methodologies to directly assess whether PWH receive comparably high-quality chronic disease management relative to their HIV-negative peers.

The proposed capstone project addresses this combined gap directly: a matched-cohort, claims-based study applying condition-specific, ACOVE-informed quality-of-care measures across ten chronic conditions common to both populations, alongside frailty (CFI) and comorbidity (Charlson, with the AIDS-weighting issue explicitly handled) measures, comparing people with and without HIV over time. In doing so, it extends an epidemiological literature that has firmly established *that* PWH age into multimorbidity earlier into a new question the field has not yet answered: whether they are being as well cared for once they get there.
