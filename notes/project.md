# project: OHDSI-3: Multimorbidity Care Quality in People with and without HIV


## Stakeholder(s)
Brianne Olivieri-Mui, PhD, MPH, Assistant Professor: Bouvé College, Affiliate Faculty: Roux Institute

## New Story - Last Updated: Oct 2
People with HIV tend to develop age-related conditions, such as diabetes, heart disease, and hypertension, much earlier than people without HIV. Because providers don't usually expect these conditions in younger patients, people with HIV may receive lower-quality care for them. Treating many conditions at once can also lead to polypharmacy (taking 5 or more types of medications) and harmful drug-drug interactions, so treating only some conditions may sometimes be the better choice. Using Komodo claims data, we will compare adults aged 50+ with HIV to a matched group without HIV (by birth year, sex, and geography), calculate yearly care quality for ten common chronic conditions, and track how quality changes over time. We will then look at how these quality patterns relate to hospitalizations and mortality, and identify risk factors for low-quality care. This is intended to be a publishable paper.

Note: Our stakeholder switched our project from OHDSI-3 (HIV Surgical Quality and Payment Models) to a new project on multimorbidity care quality in people with and without HIV. We discussed this change with Prof. Bogden in advance and got the green light to proceed.

## Story 
Surgical quality is determined by several outcomes such as readmissions, complications, and mortality. Some hospitals are limited to a bundled payment model through insurance which means insurance pays them a lump sum for a given surgery and are expected to manage the surgery and up to 30 days after surgery with that lump sum. We will look at one surgical cohort that is subject to this model, either lower extremity surgical cohort or bowel surgery cohort in hospitals receiving bundled payments compared to those using other reimbursement models and how that impacts the surgical outcomes. We will also explore subpopulations of patients such as those with HIV considered “high risk” patients. This is intended to be a publishable paper.
