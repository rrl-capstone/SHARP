# Literature Survey: Multimorbidity Care Quality in People With and Without HIV

---

## 1. HIV and Premature Aging / Multimorbidity

This cluster establishes the core justification for the study: that people with HIV develop age-related comorbidities earlier, and carry a higher multimorbidity burden, than the general population — even with modern ART.

### Premature Age-Related Comorbidities Among HIV-Infected Persons Compared With the General Population
**Citation:** Guaraldi G, Orlando G, Zona S, et al. *Clinical Infectious Diseases*. 2011;53(11):1120–1126.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/cir627
**Design:** Cross-sectional; single clinic (Modena, Italy); ART-experienced HIV+ patients vs. age/sex/ethnicity-matched HIV− controls.
**Key finding:** HIV+ patients aged 41–50 had the same rate of having 2+ concurrent conditions ("polypathology") as HIV− controls aged 51–60 — roughly a decade's difference in onset. Risk factors: lower nadir CD4 count, longer ART exposure.
**Relevance:** The original, most-cited foundational paper for this research area.

### Morbidity and Aging in HIV-Infected Persons: The Swiss HIV Cohort Study
**Citation:** Hasse B, Ledergerber B, Furrer H, et al.; Swiss HIV Cohort Study. *Clinical Infectious Diseases*. 2011;53(11):1130–1139.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/cir626
**Design:** Longitudinal, within-HIV+-cohort only (no HIV− comparator); Cox regression by age strata, adjusted for CD4, viral load, smoking, injection drug use, duration of infection.
**Key finding:** Non-AIDS morbidity (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, non-AIDS cancers, osteoporosis) increases meaningfully with age among PWH.
**Relevance:** Published in the same CID issue as Guaraldi; different cohort (Swiss vs. Italian) and different design (longitudinal incidence vs. cross-sectional prevalence) — complements rather than duplicates.

### Cross-Sectional Comparison of the Prevalence of Age-Associated Comorbidities and Their Risk Factors Between HIV-Infected and Uninfected Individuals: The AGEhIV Cohort Study
**Citation:** Schouten J, Wit FW, Stolte IG, et al.; AGEhIV Cohort Study Group. *Clinical Infectious Diseases*. 2014;59(12):1787–1797.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciu701
**Design:** Dutch (Amsterdam) cohort; 598 HIV+ vs. 550 closely matched HIV− controls, all aged 45+.
**Key finding:** HIV infection independently associated with higher comorbidity risk (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.23–2.03) even after adjusting for smoking, family history, waist-to-hip ratio.
**Relevance:** A third independent national cohort (Italy, Switzerland, Netherlands) showing the same pattern — triangulating evidence across populations.

### HIV and Aging: State of Knowledge and Areas of Critical Need for Research
**Citation:** High KP, Brennan-Ing M, Clifford DB, et al.; HIV and Aging Working Group. *Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes*. 2012;60(Suppl 1):S1–S18.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1097/QAI.0b013e31825a3668
**Design:** Consensus report, not primary data — ~20 experts across HIV medicine and geriatrics.
**Key content:** Maps the field (immunology, organ systems, frailty, neurocognitive decline, pharmacology) and flags research gaps as of 2012.
**Relevance:** Framing/background citation — shows this study addresses a gap the NIH itself identified.

### Comparison of Overall and Comorbidity-Free Life Expectancy Between Insured Adults With and Without HIV Infection, 2000–2016
**Citation:** Marcus JL, Leyden WA, Alexeeff SE, et al. *JAMA Network Open*. 2020;3(6):e207954.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2020.7954
**Design:** Kaiser Permanente insured cohort, matched 1:10 HIV+ to HIV−.
**Key finding:** Overall life-expectancy gap narrowed over the study period (to ~9 years; further narrowed with early ART initiation), but the *comorbidity-free* life expectancy gap did not improve over time — people with HIV experienced age-related comorbidities roughly 16 years earlier, attenuated to ~9.5 years with early ART.
**Relevance:** Most current/precise quantification of the premature-comorbidity effect; directly supports the framing that longevity gains have outpaced comorbidity prevention.

### Multimorbidity Patterns in People With HIV
**Citation:** de Francesco D, Sabin CA, Reiss P. *Current Opinion in HIV and AIDS*. 2020;15(2):110–117.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1097/COH.0000000000000595
**Design:** Narrative review of data-driven multimorbidity pattern studies.
**Key finding:** Despite heterogeneous methods, patterns of mental health, cardiovascular, metabolic, and musculoskeletal conditions co-occur consistently in both the general population and PWH.
**Relevance:** Review/synthesis paper — useful for framing what's already known about comorbidity clustering.

### The Increasing Burden and Complexity of Multi-Morbidity and Polypharmacy in Geriatric HIV Patients: A Cross Sectional Study of People Aged 65–74 Years and More Than 75 Years
**Citation:** Guaraldi G, Malagoli A, Calcagno A, et al. *BMC Geriatrics*. 2018;18(1):99.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-018-0789-0
**Design:** Italian GEPPO cohort; 1,258 HIV+ vs. 315 HIV− geriatric patients.
**Key finding:** Multimorbidity (64%) and polypharmacy (37%) both more prevalent in HIV+ patients; associated with duration of HIV infection rather than age itself.
**Relevance:** Directly connects comorbidity burden to polypharmacy — links two of this study's core variables.

### Geriatric Syndromes in Older HIV-Infected Adults
**Citation:** Greene M, Covinsky KE, Valcour V, et al. *Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes*. 2015;69(2):161–167.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1097/QAI.0000000000000556
**Key finding:** Among older PWH: median 4 comorbidities, median 9 non-ART medications; 53.6% had 2+ geriatric syndromes; 56.1% prefrail (Fried criteria).
**Relevance:** Connects multimorbidity and polypharmacy to frailty (this study's CFI measure) and geriatric syndromes more broadly.

### Cigarette Smoking Prevalence Among Adults With HIV Compared With the General Adult Population in the United States
**Citation:** Mdodo R, Frazier EL, Dube SR, et al. *Annals of Internal Medicine*. 2015;162(5):335–344.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-0954
**Design:** Medical Monitoring Project (HIV+, n=4,217 weighted to ~420,000) vs. 2009 NHIS general population (n=27,731).
**Key finding:** 42.4% current smoking in HIV+ vs. 20.6% in general population (~2x); lower quit rates (32.4% vs. 51.7%). Independently associated with older age, race, poverty, homelessness, incarceration history, depression, unsuppressed viral load.
**Relevance:** Supporting/confounder evidence — smoking is a shared risk factor for several Table 1 conditions (COPD, coronary artery disease).

## 2. Comorbidity and Frailty Indices

### A New Method of Classifying Prognostic Comorbidity in Longitudinal Studies: Development and Validation
**Citation:** Charlson ME, Pompei P, Ales KL, MacKenzie CR. *Journal of Chronic Diseases*. 1987;40(5):373–383.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9681(87)90171-8
**Design:** Derivation cohort of 559 medical patients; validated on 685 breast cancer patients followed 10 years; 19 weighted conditions (weights 1–6) predicting 1-year mortality.
**Key finding:** AIDS carries the single highest weight in the index (6 points, tied with metastatic solid tumor).
**Relevance:** Source of the comorbidity index used in this protocol. **Flagged issue:** since AIDS is weighted at 6 points — the maximum in the index — every HIV+ patient in our cohort receives a built-in +6 score purely by virtue of HIV status, which directly affects any HIV+ vs. HIV− comparison using this index (raised as a question for Dr. Olivieri-Mui).

### Coding Algorithms for Defining Comorbidities in ICD-9-CM and ICD-10 Administrative Data
**Citation:** Quan H, Sundararajan V, Halfon P, et al. *Medical Care*. 2005;43(11):1130–1139.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000182534.19832.83
**Key content:** Develops and validates ICD-9-CM and ICD-10 coding algorithms to operationalize Charlson and Elixhauser comorbidities from administrative/claims data. Enhanced algorithms matched or outperformed the original Deyo ICD-9-CM algorithm (C-statistics ranging ~0.84–0.88 across versions).
**Relevance:** Essential companion to Charlson 1987 — provides the actual ICD code lists needed to compute the index from Komodo claims data.

### Validation of a Patient-Level Medication Regimen Complexity Index as a Possible Tool to Identify Patients for Medication Therapy Management Intervention
**Citation:** Hirsch JD, Metz KR, Hosokawa PW, Libby AM. *Pharmacotherapy*. 2014;34(8):826–835.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/phar.1452
**Key content:** Validates the 65-item Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI) using claims data across several chronic-disease cohorts, including HIV, diabetes, and hypertension.
**Relevance:** A more granular alternative to this protocol's polypharmacy measure (5+ ATC-3 classes) — useful discussion point for the methods/limitations section.

## 3. Quality-of-Care Measurement (ACOVE)

This cluster underlies the methodology of Table 1 — the condition-specific care-quality measures are built in the same process-of-care-indicator tradition as ACOVE.

### Assessing Care of Vulnerable Elders: ACOVE Project Overview
**Citation:** Wenger NS, Shekelle PG; ACOVE Investigators. *Annals of Internal Medicine*. 2001;135(8 Pt 2):642–646.
**Link:** https://www.rand.org/health/projects/acove/pubs.html (RAND's full ACOVE publications hub — this paper is listed there; no standalone DOI found). A readable RAND summary covering ACOVE-1 through ACOVE-3 is also available at https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9320.html
**Methodology:** Developed by RAND Health and UCLA (2000, Pfizer-funded). "Vulnerable elders" operationally defined via the VES-13 survey (age, self-reported health, functional status); a score of 3+ carried 4x the risk of death or functional decline in validation. A national panel of geriatrics experts identified 22 conditions; for each, a structured literature review produced candidate process-of-care indicators, validated via the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method (multidisciplinary expert panels, two rounds of anonymous 1–9 ratings with a face-to-face discussion between rounds). Indicators span four domains of care: screening/prevention, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up/continuity.
**Key empirical finding:** When applied to real patients, only ~55% of recommended care processes were met overall — ranging from 9% (end-of-life care) to 82% (stroke care).
**Relevance:** Direct methodological ancestor of this study's Table 1 (denominator/numerator process-of-care indicator structure); the 55% figure is a ready benchmark to compare our own findings against.

### Introduction to the Assessing Care of Vulnerable Elders-3 Quality Indicator Measurement Set
**Citation:** Wenger NS, Roth CP, Shekelle P; ACOVE Investigators. *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*. 2007;55(Suppl 2):S247–S252.
**Link:** https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP20071040.html (RAND page; no standalone DOI found)
**Key content:** Expanded to 26 conditions and 392 quality indicators, same four domains of care as ACOVE-1.
**Relevance:** Direct parent publication of the condition-specific papers below.

### Quality Indicators for the Care of Heart Failure in Vulnerable Elders
**Citation:** Heidenreich PA. *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*. 2007;55(Suppl 2).
**Link:** https://www.rand.org/health/projects/acove/pubs.html (listed on RAND's ACOVE publications hub; no standalone DOI found)
**Key content:** Condition-specific ACOVE-3 process-of-care quality indicators for heart failure, developed via the same expert-panel methodology as the overview paper.
**Relevance:** Direct source for the heart failure / cardiac-care row in Table 1.

### Assessing Quality of Care of Elderly Patients Using the ACOVE Quality Indicator Set: A Systematic Review
**Citation:** Askari M, Wierenga PC, Eslami S, Medlock S. *PLOS ONE*. 2011;6(12):e28631.
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0028631
**Key finding:** Across 17 studies using ACOVE or adapted ACOVE indicators (278 QIs total), quality of care varied markedly across conditions and settings; overall quality remained relatively low despite improvement efforts.
**Relevance:** Shows ACOVE has been validated and adapted across many settings since 2001 — supports using it as a methodological basis for a new condition set rather than treating it as fixed.
